Robyn: a Royal Canadian Navy veteran’s VAC claims story

Having the skills did not make it easy to do alone

Story 01 · 3 min read Lieutenant-Commander (Ret’d), Royal Canadian Navy

Robyn knew how to research, write and work through complex documents. As a military legal officer, those skills had been central to her career.

But writing about her own health and traumatic experiences was different.

She had completed a PTSD claim herself. The process was emotionally draining, and the thought of doing it again made other applications difficult to face. What she needed was someone who could listen, organize the information, and carry the practical work forward.

A career built through determination

Robyn returned to university as a single parent raising three young children. She completed her undergraduate degree, entered law school and joined the Naval Reserves in 1999.

She later transferred to the Regular Force as a legal officer. Her work included advising military commanders, teaching the law of armed conflict and supporting operations in Canada and overseas.

She served in Bosnia and Afghanistan, where she worked with Afghan military legal personnel. Yellowknife became a particularly memorable posting, allowing her to travel widely while advising on northern operations.

She valued work that brought her face to face with the people carrying out the mission.

Robyn was medically released on her 60th birthday in May 2024.

The emotional cost of writing it all down

PTSD, anxiety and physical health problems were affecting Robyn’s daily life.

Completing her first claim meant gathering medical records and describing deeply personal experiences, including military sexual assault. Revisiting those experiences affected her sleep, thoughts, and ability to cope.

Her difficulty was not a lack of knowledge or writing ability. It was the emotional weight of using those skills to set out what had happened to her.

At the Lawrencetown exhibition, our compass rose logo caught her attention. She stopped to ask about the company and later spoke with us.

Her request was direct:

“Please take this off my plate.”

Turning a conversation into clear documentation

Robyn began working with Tanya, a benefits navigator. Through conversations, she could explain her experiences without having to construct every part of the written application herself.

When she reviewed the prepared claims, she felt the information had been organized clearly and reflected effects she had sometimes downplayed.

That helped her acknowledge the limitations she was living with.

We also arranged assessments with a nurse practitioner. Robyn had been without a family physician since returning to Nova Scotia, and obtaining the required medical documentation had been another obstacle.

The team coordinated the assessments and the additional work needed when something required a further visit.

For Robyn, having someone carry that practical and emotional burden had value beyond the eventual decision.

“Even if I hadn’t been successful, it still would have been invaluable.”

Conversation arrangements respected her capacity

Robyn’s ability to manage conversations and appointments varied from day to day.

She appreciated being able to meet in person, speak by phone or use email and text.

“The means of communication was up to me.”

The flexibility allowed her to communicate in a way she could manage. Tanya also checked in about how she was doing, rather than contacting her only when a document needed attention.

Robyn remembers discussing the pain in her hands and later receiving a wax-treatment kit from Tanya. She describes that gesture as a sign that her concerns had been heard.

“I didn’t feel like a number. I didn’t feel like a file.”

Support beyond the disability claims

Robyn subsequently received approvals for several physical conditions. We also helped her complete applications for rehabilitation, income replacement and the Veterans Independence Program.

While some health-related matters remained outstanding, Robyn nevertheless described substantial changes in her life.

Compensation had allowed her to pay off her home and help her children. Other approved supports were helping with practical needs at home, including bathroom adaptations and household tasks.

She also speaks warmly about her VAC caseworkers and rehabilitation specialist.

The combined support reduced some of her anxiety about how she would manage as she grew older. She no longer felt responsible for filing every application, appointment and follow-up herself.

Robyn’s story shows that knowing how to do something does not always make it possible to do alone. For her, having someone take on the practical work made it easier to pursue support while attending to her health and daily life.
