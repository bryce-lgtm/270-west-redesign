A disability benefits decision can raise questions even when your claim is approved. What, exactly, is the payment recognizing? Why was that amount assigned? Should you keep the monthly payment or consider a lump sum?

VAC Pain and Suffering Compensation (PSC) recognizes the effects of a service-related disability on your well-being and daily life. It is a tax-free benefit, paid monthly unless you choose to cash out the remaining value. To make sense of your decision letter, look at three things in order: what condition was accepted, how its effects were assessed, and what payment the decision provides.

What does the benefit recognize?

Despite its name, the VAC Pain and Suffering Compensation is not limited to physical pain. It recognizes non-economic effects of a service-related disability, including physical or psychological loss, reduced ability to function and changes to quality of life. It is different from a benefit intended to replace lost employment income.

Think of a knee condition that makes stairs, walking or attending appointments difficult. Or a mental health condition that affects concentration, coping or everyday routines. The relevant question is not simply “What is the diagnosis?” It is also “What are the documented effects of this condition?”

Before an amount is set, what does an application need to show?

An application needs information about the diagnosed condition, its connection to service and its effects. VAC reviews materials such as medical reports or questionnaires and service health records. The precise information needed depends on the condition and the claim.

Consider a veteran applying for a persistent back condition. A diagnosis identifies the condition. Service records and other relevant information help explain its connection to service. Medical findings and descriptions of daily activities help show how the condition affects the veteran now. Those pieces answer different questions, and one does not replace the others.

The same principle applies to mental health conditions, although the relevant records and effects may differ. You do not need to make your experience sound worse than it is. Specific, accurate information gives the decision-maker a clearer picture than broad statements such as “it affects everything.”

How is VAC Pain and Suffering Compensation determined?

Three factors shape the percentage used to calculate Pain and Suffering Compensation:

  1. Entitlement: How much of the disability is connected to service? Entitlement may be full or partial.
  2. Medical impairment rating: How does the accepted condition affect physical or mental functioning? VAC uses the relevant section of its Table of Disabilities to assign a rating.
  3. Quality-of-life rating: How does the condition affect everyday activities, relationships, and recreational or community life? VAC assigns a quality-of-life level, then uses the medical impairment rating to convert that level into a rating.

VAC adds the medical impairment and quality-of-life ratings. If entitlement is partial, it then applies that degree of entitlement to the combined figure. This produces the disability assessment for the condition, which contributes to the percentage used to calculate your PSC payment.

When reading your decision letter, look for both ratings and any partial entitlement before comparing the final percentage with VAC’s rate table. The medical impairment rating alone is not the complete assessment.

Monthly payments and lump sums: what should you compare?

After a favourable PSC decision, payment begins monthly. You can later choose to take the remaining value of your PSC as one lump-sum payment instead of continuing monthly payments. You must make this choice in writing, and you cannot change it afterward. It also applies to all the monthly PSC you receive at that time, not just one condition.

VAC’s published monthly rates show how the two payment formats compare. At 20% on its rate table, the figures effective January 1, 2026 are $283.96 per month or $92,391.34 as a lump sum. Those figures illustrate the table. They are not a personal cash-out quote. Your entitlement, payments already received and other applicable circumstances can affect your amount. Rates also change over time.

When comparing Veterans Affairs lump-sum payments with monthly PSC, ask for your current individual figures:

  • What monthly amount does my decision award?
  • What lump-sum amount remains if I choose to cash out now?
  • What would it mean for me to give up this ongoing monthly payment?

The decision is personal. Understanding the amount you would receive and the fact that the choice cannot be reversed matters more than choosing quickly.

How does PSC account for mental health conditions?

There is no set PSC amount for a mental health diagnosis. For an accepted psychiatric condition, VAC considers its effects on thought and concentration, emotion and coping, daily activities, treatment needs and quality of life. Two people with the same diagnosis may experience those effects differently.

As you read your own records, ask: Do they name the diagnosis but leave out what daily life is like? If symptoms vary, describe the pattern: what happens, how often, and what support you need. An accurate account is more useful than describing only your best or hardest day.

You have a decision. What should you do next?

Start by reading the letter for the accepted condition, degree of entitlement, assessment and payment amount. If something does not match your understanding, note the exact part of the letter and the information you want to discuss. The next step depends on what has changed, or what you disagree with:

  • A different condition has developed: A new application may be appropriate, including where you believe the new condition is connected to a previously accepted one.
  • An accepted condition has worsened since its assessment: A reassessment may be appropriate if medical information shows the change.
  • You disagree with the original decision: Review and appeal options may be available. Start with the reasons in your decision letter and identify the information or finding you disagree with.

These are different paths because they address different questions. A later change in health is not the same as saying the original decision missed information or reached the wrong conclusion.

Common questions about VAC Pain and Suffering Compensation

Can I apply while I am still serving?

Yes. Eligible Canadian Armed Forces members and veterans can receive PSC for a disability that meets the program’s requirements.

Is Pain and Suffering Compensation taxable?

No. PSC is a tax-free benefit recognizing the non-economic effects of a service-related disability.

Is Additional Pain and Suffering Compensation the same benefit?

No. Additional Pain and Suffering Compensation is a separate benefit for eligible veterans whose severe, permanent service-related impairment creates a barrier to life after service. It has its own requirements and assessment. Receiving a disability benefit does not make the additional benefit automatic.

Do I need to choose a lump sum when my claim is approved?

No. PSC begins as a monthly payment after a favourable decision. You can choose to take the remaining value as a lump sum later. If you have already received monthly payments, VAC takes them into account when calculating that amount.
