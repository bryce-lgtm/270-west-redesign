I joined the Naval Reserve in Halifax in 1999, and I loved it. A few years later I moved to the regular force and became a legal officer. I spent most of my career as a deputy judge advocate, working face to face with commanders on everything from operations to discipline. I deployed to Bosnia, and to Afghanistan for nine and a half months, where I mentored judges and prosecutors. Not bad for a little girl from Sackville, Nova Scotia.

Then my health caught up with me. I developed AFib, which triggered PTSD I didn’t know I had. I couldn’t do my job, I was put on work limitations, and I felt abandoned by the military. I was medically released on May 24, 2024, my 60th birthday.

I’m a lawyer. I’m more than capable of filing my own claims, and I did file my PTSD claim myself, but only because VAC told me to finish it or they would close the file. Describing how my condition affects my daily life, for people I would never meet, brought everything back to the surface. The nightmares, the intrusive thoughts, the sleepless nights. There were other things I could have claimed years earlier and just couldn’t bring myself to do. I tried five times to complete one application and never got through it.

About a year ago I wandered past a booth at the Lawrencetown exhibition. The compass rose logo caught my eye, being a Navy person. Within 48 hours someone had reached out. What I wanted was simple: please take this off my plate. The commission structure made sense to me as well. Much like a lawsuit, they only succeed if I do, so they have a dog in the fight.

When I first read my claims, the wordsmithing was amazing. As veterans, we downplay everything. Are you hurt or are you injured? We always think we’re hurt. Seeing my situation written out as a complete package helped me accept that I’m not broken, but I certainly am dented in a few places. They also arranged every medical assessment. I’ve been without a family doctor for over two years, and I would have been dead in the water without that.

Every claim I’ve submitted with 270 West has been approved: both ankles, both knees, my lower spine, my hands, my income replacement benefit and additional pain and suffering. I’m now in VAC’s vocational rehabilitation program and approved for the VIP program at home. Only my cardiac issue and sleep apnea are still outstanding, waiting on specialist input. I’m now considered over 100% disabled, which means I won’t have to fight for support as I age.

What I keep coming back to is how personal it was. Tanya called me before the impersonal email arrived to tell me my last claim was approved. When I mentioned my hands had been bad, a hot wax kit showed up a few days later. Tanya also helped with my vocational rehab and VIP applications, and there was no fee for that. I could reach them by phone, in person, by email or by text, whichever I had the energy for that day.

I have a lot more financial security now, and it has let me breathe. But the biggest change is that I don’t feel abandoned anymore. My service had value, and I’m worth taking care of.
