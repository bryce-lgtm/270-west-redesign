I joined the Navy in 1993, fresh out of high school at 18. I wanted out of my hometown, and two cousins already in the military had told me what to expect. I served for three years. It wasn’t the life I had pictured. Some of what happened in those years was wrong, and I carried it for a long time. But I also learned discipline, and a lot about people. I left in 1996, married, and raised two kids. Today I work in finance as a mortgage broker.

I injured my knee on a training course in Victoria and had surgery in the military. Sixteen years after I got out, a friend messaged me on Facebook and asked if I had ever claimed a pension for that knee. I hadn’t known I could. So I filed on my own, with an 18-page form that I took to a doctor who had no idea how to fill it out. Things got rejected and sent back. It took years, and to this day I don’t know whether I got the right amount.

Four or five years ago I was sponsoring the Valley Maple Leafs, and 270 West had the booth next to ours. My husband said these were people I could refer military clients to. What neither of us realized was that I could refer myself. When I mentioned a past lawsuit related to my service, the benefits navigator told me there was way more available, including medical benefits and counselling.

That first meeting was at the Dartmouth office. I told them about my life, and they said we would look at this, and this, and this. I was surprised by how many things I had lived with for years were claimable conditions connected to my service. Then there was the stuff I had never known about. A monthly non-taxable pension was available by checking a box. I also had coverage for physio, massage and chiropractic for my knee, and I had spent so much on those out of pocket over the past 15 years.

They didn’t feed me with a fire hose. We started with a few claims, then moved on to the next as each one was finished. The navigators are veterans, so they can relate. Nobody pushed me to say more than I wanted to. They set up the right practitioners, people who know how to fill out the forms, and they made sure I got in quickly.

When we started, there was no promise of payment. They were working for free until I got paid, and I never felt taken advantage of. If anything, I felt looked after. Many of my clients struggle to get their disability tax credit because a doctor doesn’t know how to fill out the form. People know they’re entitled, but the paperwork was done wrong. That’s what I see happening to veterans.

Looking ahead, I have a bad knee that grinds every day, and one day I will probably need a replacement. Knowing that I’ll be looked after when that time comes is huge. If you’ve been through service and think it hasn’t affected you, it can come back years later. Getting your claims in now gives you a baseline.
