I joined the Canadian Armed Forces in 1984 as a supply technician, and later became a naval logistics officer. Over 33 years I served in six ships, with Special Operations Forces Command, and on six overseas tours, including the Persian Gulf and the NATO fleet. I got out in 2017. Leaving is hard. The uniform is part of your identity, and when you take your tunic off for the last time, it feels like taking off part of who you are.

I had one injury that never went away, a shoulder injury from basic officer training in Chilliwack. It was there every day, worse after cutting the lawn. So I went on the VAC website, worked out how to submit a claim, and wrote it myself. I consider myself a good writer. It was denied. I appealed. Denied again. I went to my member of parliament, who wasn’t any help. After three tries, I gave up. A denial feels like an attack on your credibility, as if they don’t believe you were hurt.

Then I stopped by a 270 West tent at Family Days in the dockyard in Halifax. I’m not a shy person, so I had a chat, and eventually I sat down for a first meeting with a benefits navigator. She understood every acronym I threw at her, and she made me comfortable enough to talk about things I hadn’t shared with many people. I went home and told my wife this was the real deal.

I walked in thinking only about my shoulder. Through her questions, we also identified my wrist, my knee, and my back. I’d assumed my sore back was just me being 62. She told me bad backs are common among people who serve in ships, from years of compression in rough weather. We wrote the claim, the questionnaire came back, I got an X-ray, and VAC awarded it. She also suggested I talk to a mental health professional after hearing about my deployments. What I expected to take one or two sessions took six, and we found real ways to improve my health.

The difference in effort was huge. On my own, I spent hours and hours writing and editing to fit the form. With my navigator, I had one hour-long session, and she came back with everything written. I still reviewed it and had full control over what went to VAC. She also sent me to health-care providers who understood service injuries, and I didn’t have to coordinate any of it. I now have coverage for regular visits to a registered massage therapist, which has been life-changing.

The fee is 10% of the award, and only if the claim succeeds. That was okay with me, because I focused on the 90% I wouldn’t have had if I’d tried alone. When I tried alone, I got nothing.

It isn’t only business, either. Tanya knew I was driving to Ottawa for a Leafs game, and she texted afterward to see if I was okay because Toronto lost. That’s a small thing, but there’s a lot of friendship in this process too.

I recently spent a weekend at Ribfest in Greenwood talking to veterans, and the first question was always whether 270 West is legitimate. I tell them I had the same worry. My advice is to take the first meeting. It costs an hour. That’s it.
