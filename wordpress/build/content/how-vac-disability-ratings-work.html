A VAC disability rating is a percentage assigned to a service-related condition that Veterans Affairs Canada has accepted. It is not a score for how much pain you feel or a judgment about your overall health.

It brings together several things: which condition was recognized, its connection to service, its medical effects and its impact on everyday life.

The clearest way to understand VAC disability ratings is to separate those parts. Once you know what the decision says about each one, you can judge whether the rating reflects the information in your file and what question, if any, needs to be addressed next.

Start with the condition the decision recognizes

Before focusing on the percentage, find the entitlement decision in your letter. Entitlement is the decision about whether your diagnosed condition is connected to military or RCMP service. That connection may be recognized fully or partially. The disability rating addresses a different question: the assessed extent of the accepted condition. Both entitlement and assessment affect the benefit calculation.

For example, your letter may accept a back condition as service-related, but the rating may not seem to reflect the limits described in your medical records. You may want to look more closely at the assessment. If the letter grants only partial entitlement, you may want to understand how the connection to service was decided. Knowing which part concerns you helps you focus on the relevant reasons and evidence in the letter.

What goes into a disability rating?

The rating draws on two assessments: medical impairment and quality of life. VAC’s Table of Disabilities sets out how they are considered for an accepted condition.

Medical impairment

This part looks at the condition’s effect on the relevant body part and your ability to function. Different conditions are assessed under different table chapters, each with its own criteria. A diagnosis names the condition; it does not, on its own, establish how much function has been affected.

That is why two veterans with the same diagnosis may not receive the same rating. The assessment depends on the effects documented in each person’s case and the criteria that apply.

Quality of life

Here, “quality of life” has a specific meaning. It is how the accepted condition affects independent living, recreation, community activities and personal relationships. It is not a judgment about how well you cope.

Think about an ordinary week. Can you get to appointments, manage household tasks and take part in the activities and relationships that are important to you? What now requires more effort, changes to your routine or another person’s help? VAC’s quality-of-life criteria describe mild, moderate and extreme levels of impact in these areas.

The level is converted to a number using the medical impairment rating. The two figures are then combined. Here is an example of how the two parts fit together:

  1. The condition receives a 20% medical impairment rating.
  2. Its effect on daily life falls under Level 2, meaning moderate impact. Level 2 does not mean “add 2%.”
  3. In VAC’s conversion table, the row that includes 20% says to add 3 percentage points for Level 2.

The disability assessment is therefore 23%, which is 20% plus 3%.

This example assumes full entitlement. Partial entitlement involves a further adjustment, and these made-up figures do not predict anyone’s rating.

How to help the assessment reflect your experience

You do not need to calculate a percentage yourself. What you can do is check whether the information about your condition is complete and specific enough to be assessed. Before an application or reassessment is submitted, ask:

  • Is each condition clearly identified? Check that the name of the condition in the application agrees with the medical information. If you are applying for several conditions, describe each one separately.
  • Do the reports explain more than the diagnosis? Medical reports and any requested questionnaires should provide information about the condition and its effects. For a reassessment, the important question is what has changed since the earlier assessment.
  • Could someone unfamiliar with your day picture the difficulty? “Travel is hard” gives little detail. Needing a driver for appointments, or repeated breaks on a familiar journey, explains what has changed.
  • Is the pattern clear? If symptoms vary, describe how often difficulties occur and what a usual period looks like, not only a particularly good or difficult day.
  • Have you reviewed everything together? Look for unclear dates, condition names or descriptions before approving the application for submission.

These checks cannot secure a particular rating. They help present the information needed for an accurate assessment. This is one of the many supports we provide at 270 West: organizing the information, reviewing it with you and helping submit it with your approval.

What if more than one condition is involved?

Several diagnoses do not always produce several separate percentages. Conditions are generally assessed under the relevant table chapters. When accepted conditions affect the same area or their effects cannot be separated medically, they may be assessed together as “bracketed conditions.” The point to check is whether the effects of your accepted conditions have been accounted for. Disability benefits are payable up to a maximum assessment of 100%.

What does the percentage mean for payment?

A rating is not a dollar amount. Payment also depends on the degree of entitlement and which benefit applies. Pain and Suffering Compensation has monthly and lump-sum options; Disability Pensions follow different rules. For amounts, check VAC’s current rates. The percentage does not decide eligibility for every other VAC program.

Read your decision letter with these questions in mind

Keep the letter in front of you and find:

  1. The condition: Which condition was decided on?
  2. The service connection: Was entitlement full or partial?
  3. The assessment: What percentage was assigned?
  4. The explanation: Which evidence and reasons were given? What review or appeal options does the letter describe?

You may agree with one part of the decision and have questions about another. Identifying that part makes your next conversation with VAC more useful.

Which next step fits your situation?

  • The original decision may have missed relevant evidence: Find out whether a departmental review is appropriate. Other review and appeal options may also apply.
  • An accepted condition has worsened since its assessment: With medical evidence of the change, ask about a reassessment.
  • A different condition has developed: It may need a new application rather than a reassessment of the existing one.

These are starting points for understanding the routes, not predictions of a decision. We can help you read the letter, work out which question needs an answer and prepare the relevant information. VAC makes the decision; you remain involved in what goes forward.

Other questions you may have about your VAC disability rating

Can two veterans with the same diagnosis have different ratings?

Yes. The diagnosis identifies the condition. The rating also depends on its documented effects under the relevant table criteria, including its impact on quality of life.

Does a 0% rating mean my condition was denied?

Not necessarily. A nil, or 0%, assessment can mean there was no measurable impairment under the applicable criteria at the time. Check the entitlement finding separately to see whether the condition was accepted.

Can I request another assessment if my condition gets worse?

Yes. If an accepted condition has worsened and medical evidence supports the change, you can ask VAC about reassessment. A higher rating is not automatic.

Do I need to suggest a percentage when I apply?

No. Describe the condition and its effects accurately and provide the required medical information. The percentage is determined using the Table of Disabilities.
